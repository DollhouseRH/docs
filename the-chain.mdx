## One line, not a tree

Dollhouse has exactly one chain.

```
(ETH on Pons) → $DOLL → #1 → #2 → #3 → #4 → ...
```

There is always one head, the newest coin in the chain, and exactly one next spot open. Every round competes for that one spot.

A branching family tree was considered and rejected: branches split attention, and a branch nobody watches leaves its holders with nothing downstream.

## Every coin is paired with its parent

This is the part that makes Dollhouse different from a normal launchpad.

Coin #3's pool does not hold $DOLL. It holds **#2**. Coin #2's pool holds **#1**. Coin #1's pool holds **$DOLL**. There is no pool in Dollhouse that holds ETH: $DOLL is adopted, not minted here, and its own ETH market sits on Pons, outside the chain.

So "priced in the coin before it" is literal. #3's price is quoted in #2, and #2's price is quoted in #1.

## Why that links liquidity

**To buy a child you must first hold its parent.** You get $DOLL on Pons, then inside Dollhouse $DOLL buys #1, #1 buys #2, #2 buys #3. Every one of those coin-to-coin trades is a real trade on a real pool.

**A child's pool holds its parent permanently.** When a coin's curve sells its supply, what comes back into the pool is parent coin, and that parent coin is locked there forever. A successful child becomes a permanent holder of its parent.

**Prices propagate.** When a child market moves, arbitrage pushes the change back up through the parent markets.

Fee sharing alone cannot reproduce any of this. That is why coins are paired against each other instead of each holding its own plain ETH pool.

## The cost of depth

Being deep in the chain has a real price, and it is not the fee.

The 1% is charged once. The per-hop pool fee is 7.5 basis points, so a ten-link route pays about 1.75% in total fees and a hundred-link route about 8.5%.

**Price impact is the real problem.** On a constant-size trade, the effective loss on a full-line route grows fast with depth, real at ten links and severe at a hundred.

Each new link has also measured out at roughly **5–8% of its parent's value** in $DOLL terms. The chain shrinks in $DOLL terms as it grows.

<Warning>
A deployment can set a permanent depth cap at launch, so the chain stays inside the range the design was tested for. The cap is fixed at deploy and can never be raised.
</Warning>

## What "canonical" means

A coin is **canonical** if it holds a numbered spot in the chain.

Canonical means exactly three things:

1. Its number and its parent are written on-chain, once, and can never be rewritten.
2. Coins in the following round are priced in it while it is the head.
3. It receives a share of the ancestor payouts on everything launched after it, forever.

<Warning>
**Canonical status guarantees nothing else.** It is not an endorsement, not a safety review, not a promise of volume, and not a promise of price. A canonical coin can fall to zero the day after it wins, and its own price still depends on the price of its parent.
</Warning>

## What canonical status costs to take

This is disclosed openly because it is uncomfortable.

Winning a round is a **capital-rental auction**. You can cycle money through your own candidate, win, and take the money back out. That costs a small fraction of the capital cycled and reliably captures the slot at a strong edge over the honest leader's absorption.

It gets cheaper with depth: clearing the minimum bar costs roughly $100 at generation 1, $7 at generation 2, and under $1 from generation 3 onward. From there, the forfeited bond is the main remaining deterrent: a flat amount on mainnet, the same at every depth.

There is no fix for this that the project was willing to ship: every structural fix (a sunk cost, a sell lock, a seasoning period) was rejected as hostile to ordinary users. So it is disclosed instead.
